# <font color="#**FFFF00**">1) Introduction Summary</font>
- The [[digital economy]] has become increasingly central to global capitalism and its [[accumulation]], and therefore, it must be understood through the lends of its economic imperatives rather than merely culturally (2-3)
## <font color="#ff0000">Key Ideas</font>
1. The technology sector remains a relatively small part of the U.S. economy, accounting for around 6.8% of value added from private companies and employs 2.5% of the labor force, compared to deindustrialized U.S. which employs four times as many (3-4). Tech companies are notoriously small, Google (60,000), Facebook (12,000), WhatsApp (55 before 19 billion dollar Facebook sale), Instagram (13 before 1 bilion dollar facebook sale) (4)
2. The digital economy cuts across various spheres of production (manufacturing, services, transportation, mining, telecommunications) and is becoming essential to much of the economy, i.e., part of the [[infrastructure|general conditions of production]]. It is increasingly important because 1) its the most dynamic sector, based on constant innovation, 2) its becoming more systematically  important as did finance, becoming 'pervasive infrastructure' for the contemporary economy 3) its dynamism is an ideal that legitimizes capitalism, and its model is becoming hegemonic (5) 
3. Main argument of the book: Long decline in manufacturing has led to the turn toward data as a way to maintain economic growth during a secular stagnation. The [[platform economy]] has emerged as a means of extracting and controlling immense amounts of data, and these firms are increasingly monopolistic. The economy is increasingly domianted by the dynamics of these firm.

# <font color="#**FFFF00**">2) Chapter 1: The Long Downturn</font>
-  There are three moments in particular that are critical for understanding the current age of 'platform capitalism': the response to the 1970s downturn, the boom and bust of the 1990s and the response to the 2008 crisis (9). 
## <font color="#ff0000">Key Ideas</font>
1. The "gold age" of capitalism, defined by high growth and productivity, was an anomaly, and the increasing competition between the U.S., Japan and German in manufacturing eventually led to a decline in profitability, and a general crises. American firms attempted to get out of this by 1) copying the lean productive models of their global competitors and 2) attacking organized labor, reorienting the labor process by both contracting and outsourcing labor (15-17)
2. The 1990s "internet bubble" was driven by the financial speculation of [[venture capital]] in the [[telecommunication]] sector (20). At its height 1% of GDP was tech companies, there were 50,000 formed to commercialize the internet and more than 256 billion in capital provided, and during the peak between 1997-2000, tech stocks rose 300 per cent to a market cap of 5 trillion . These firms increasingly took a "growth before profits" model, in that they did not have much revenue but were hoping to gain a foothold in the market (21). The massive level of investment in the sector was at the time unprecedented, with billions being spend on computing infrastructure, e.g., million of miles of fiber optic submarine cables (22)
3. The 2008 crises led to further structural systems in the economy, [[quantitative easing]] saw central banks (U.S., U.K, E.U) purchasing government bonds to flood the financial system with liquidity (12.3 trillion was purchased by 2016). By reducing the supply of government bonds, this increased the supply of money looking for alternative assets, lowering the interest rates of other assets (which are now invested in with this new cash), and leading to the inflation of corporate asset prices (28-29). Crucially, the generalized lower interest rates led to reduced rates of returns on various financial assets, spurring for people to seek higher returns in increasingly risky assets, **for example unprofitable and unproven tech companies**. The crises has also created an increasing proletarianization and surplus population for capital to exploit.

# <font color="#**FFFF00**">3) Chapter 2:  Platform Capitalism</font>
- Data extraction as a means of accumulation has become central to 21st century capitalism, and this holds for all five forms of 'platform capitalism', i.e., the [[advertising platforms]] ([[Google]] and [[Facebook]]), the [[cloud platforms]] ([[Amazon]] Web Services and Salesforce), [[industrial platforms]] (GE and Siemens), the [[product platforms]] (Rolls Royce and Spotify) and finally the [[lean platforms]] (Uber and Airbnb). 
## <font color="#ff0000">Key Ideas</font>
1. Cotemporary capitalistm centered around data extraction, specifically of information on *what* has happened (39). Information on past events is recorded in a physical medium, meaning sensors are used to capture data and massive systems store and maintain it. Collection and analysis of data are not immaterial or frictionless but require material infrastructures, use of inputs like energy, and practices that sense, record, and analyze the data to be used in various ways. The activities of users is the [[raw material]] that is [[extractivism|extracted]] by [[platform economy|firms]] in the modern economy.
2. New business model emerged out of the increasing social necessity of using data for the competitive pressures of [[capital accumulation]],   the [[platform economy|platform]] (42). They are 1) digital infrastructures that allow groups to interact, which 2) produce and reply on a network effect between users  from 3) the architecture oft the platform they design and control which is 4) cross-subsidized by their other profit-making activities (48)
3. The rise of these platform types (ignoring the lean platform which is more short-lived), speaks to changing structural trends in capitalism (92). This includes for example the move to turn the mere production of [[goods]] into [[services]]. It also sees the [[concentration of capital|concentration of owernership]], as firms are building large [[infrastructure]] and acquiring competitors. 

# <font color="#**FFFF00**">4) Chapter 3: Great Platform Wars</font>
- Platform capitalism has various tendencies as a result of its business model, and while they do produce some monopolisation of particular infrastructure, this is in the context of increasingly intense generalised competition. However, there are challenges to the model becoming a driver of grow (depending on which specific model), in particular that it overall reduces costs and down time, is fundamentally unprofitable, and is based on easy surplus money in the economy. 
## <font color="#ff0000">Key Ideas</font>
1. There's an increase in inter-capitalist competition with the data/platform economy, and while there's a tendency toward monpolization, the corporate form, emergence of financial sophistication and monetary resources give also the conditions to topple existing monopolies (96). They are increasingly pushed to invest in [[fixed capital]] because that is the basis of their ability to extract, analyse and control [[data]] (101). 
2. The basis of the business model on extraction and network effects creates certain [[tendencies]] that emerge from the competitive dynamics of platforms, including the 1) expansion of extraction, 2) positioning as gatekeepers, 3) convergence of markets, and enclosure of ecosystems (98). 
3. There are various challenges to platform capitalism activing as a source of growth for manufacturing in capitalism, including that it reduces costs and downtime, takes surplus from manufacturing (116), its basis on outsourcing which cannot coopt skill labor which requires training (118), and finally, it is based on surplus [[money]] capital being pushed into the economy (120).
